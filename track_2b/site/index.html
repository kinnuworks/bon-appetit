Your Bon knows what you eat.

A Bon is a Swiss till receipt. Bon·Appétit reads yours with Apertus, Switzerland's open language model, and shows what your household ate this week on the Swiss food pyramid. It all happens on your own computer.

The problem

Receipts are written for the till.

Shops cut product names to fit a narrow paper roll, in the shop's language, with house-brand codes in front. The Migros Cumulus export uses the same names and has no barcodes, so there is nothing to look up.

Each line becomes a food from the federal Swiss Food Composition Database, with a confidence score. When two foods fit equally well, you get a question.

How it works

Search finds candidates. Apertus picks one.

Search proposes foods from the FSVO database and Open Food Facts. Apertus chooses one or abstains and asks you, and a symbolic food layer checks the choice. Code does every number; Apertus never writes one.

Reading

Apertus reads the shorthand.

Each line goes to Apertus 8B together with a short list of candidate foods. It expands the abbreviations and picks the candidate that fits. If two fit, it says so.

The candidate list comes from lexical search plus Swiss multilingual embeddings derived from Apertus by Andreas Martin. A line Apertus abstains on goes to your review queue with one plain question.

Checking

A food layer checks every pick.

Before a link counts, a set of plain rules looks at it. The unit has to fit the food, so litres of cheese are rejected. Bags, detergent and bottle deposits are set aside.

The energy on file has to agree with the Atwater sum: about 4 kcal per gram of protein and of carbohydrate, 9 per gram of fat. Quantities come from the line itself, converted by code.

Privacy

Air-gapped by default.

A year of shopping says a lot about health, religion and money. Under the revised Swiss data protection act that is sensitive personal data, so it stays on your machine.

Bon·Appétit runs in Docker with network_mode: none, and a test fails if a single packet leaves the container. There is no account and no telemetry. One button deletes everything. If you would rather use a Swiss cloud, point LLM_BASE_URL at it.

Around the model

The parts that make it usable.

Migros CSV and Coop PDF

Import the Cumulus CSV export or a Coop PDF receipt as the shop hands it to you.

Paste it or photograph it

Paste receipt text, or take a photo of a paper Bon. The OCR runs locally too.

Four languages

Receipts in German, French and Italian. Questions and suggestions in those three and English.

A review queue that learns

Answer a question once. Your household's names for things are remembered the next week.

The weekly pyramid

Shares of the Swiss food pyramid per adult-equivalent, with sugar, salt, saturated fat, fruit and vegetable portions and the ultra-processed share.

Swaps from the same shop

Two or three suggestions a week, in your language, from what that shop sells. Code fills in the numbers.

Delete everything

One button removes every receipt, answer and result from your computer.

The bonlink package

The receipt-line linker is its own package, so other projects can use it without the rest of the app.

Fixes for open data

A list of Open Food Facts entries whose printed values fail the energy check, ready for volunteers to correct.

Evidence

Tested against printed labels.

Baselines.

Fuzzy string matching, the way a 2018 hackathon app did it. Embeddings alone. Apertus alone, asked to guess the nutrients with no database. Then the full hybrid.

The oracle row is the best score any FSVO match could reach. FSVO holds generic foods and the packs are specific brands, so even a perfect link has some error.

Limits.

Purchases are not intake. Food gets shared, thrown away or eaten outside the house, and a receipt shows none of that.

The rules that turn product names into receipt-style truncations are synthetic, and the report documents them. FSVO values are generic, so a brand's recipe can differ.

Prior work.

TooSweet, from the Open Food Hackdays in 2018, ranked Cumulus purchases by sugar. ETH Zurich's FoodCoach and Receipt2Nutrition research studied nutrition from shopping data before us.

Bon·Appétit adds a local model that can abstain, and a check layer behind it.

Who it is for

Households first, then the people who help them.

Households

See what a week of shopping adds up to, privately, with swaps you can make in the same shop.

Dietitians and home care

Start from what a client actually bought, with their consent, instead of a food diary written from memory.

Insurer prevention programmes

Offer members a weekly view that stays on their own computer, so the receipts never reach the insurer.

Retailers' loyalty apps

Run the linker next to the purchase data a loyalty programme already holds, with the Swiss pyramid as the reference.

Cantonal health promotion

Use the open code in nutrition programmes in German, French or Italian speaking cantons.

Researchers

Reuse bonlink and the label-based evaluation to study shopping data and diet.

Run it

Three commands on your own computer.

You need git and Docker. Everything runs in a container on your machine.

Terminal
$ git clone https://github.com/kinnuworks/bon-appetit
$ cd bon-appetit/track_2b
$ make run

Apertus 8B runs locally. To check the air gap yourself, run make airgap.

Swiss cloud instead
$ export LLM_NAME=swiss-ai/Apertus-v1.5-8B
$ export LLM_BASE_URL=https://your-swiss-endpoint/v1
$ export LLM_API_KEY=your-key
$ make run

With these set, receipt lines go to that endpoint instead of the local model. Pick a provider that runs in Switzerland under Swiss law.

Questions

Honest answers.

Is this medical advice?

No. It describes what a household bought and compares that with the Swiss dietary recommendations. For a diagnosis or a diet plan, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian.

Does anything leave my computer?

Not when you run it locally. The container has no network, and a test fails if a packet gets out. There is no account and no telemetry. If you set LLM_BASE_URL, the receipt lines go to that endpoint, so choose one you trust.

Why not just use barcodes?

The Migros Cumulus export has no barcodes, and a paper receipt does not print them. The shortened product name is all there is to work with.

What about Coop?

Coop PDF receipts import directly. Lines with Coop house brands such as Naturaplan, Qualité & Prix and Prix Garantie go through the same reader as Migros lines.

What if it gets an item wrong?

Every line shows the food it was linked to and a confidence score, so a wrong link is easy to spot. When Apertus is unsure it asks instead of guessing, the food layer rejects picks that fail its checks, and your answers are remembered for the next week.

Which model runs it?

Apertus v1.5 8B, the open model from the Swiss AI Initiative, running on your machine. It reads lines and chooses between candidates. Every quantity and nutrient value is computed by code from the databases.

Can I use it in French or Italian?

Yes. It reads receipts in German, French and Italian, and asks its questions and gives its suggestions in German, French, Italian or English.

How accurate is it?

The evidence section above compares its estimates with the nutrition labels printed on Swiss products. Keep in mind that what a household buys is not exactly what it eats.
