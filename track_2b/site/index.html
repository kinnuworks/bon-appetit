Your Bon knows what you eat.

A Bon is a Swiss till receipt. Bon·Appétit reads yours with Apertus, Switzerland's open language model, and shows what your household ate this week on the Swiss food pyramid. It all happens on your own computer.

The problem

Receipts are written for the till.

Shops cut product names to fit a narrow paper roll, in the shop's language, with house-brand codes in front. The Migros Cumulus export uses the same names and has no barcodes, so there is nothing to look up.

Each line becomes a food from the federal Swiss Food Composition Database. When no entry fits, the line waits for you instead of being guessed.

How it works

Search finds candidates. Apertus picks one.

Search proposes foods from the FSVO database. Apertus reads the line, then picks one candidate by its number or answers 0, and a symbolic food layer checks the pick. Code does every number; Apertus never writes one.

Reading

Apertus reads the shorthand.

Apertus 8B is called twice per line. First it writes the line out in plain English, names the language, says whether it is food and gives a generic name in the style of the German food table. Then it sees a short list of candidates and answers with the number of the one that fits, or 0.

The candidates come from all 1,145 FSVO foods, found by lexical token matching plus Swiss multilingual embeddings derived from Apertus by Andreas Martin. A line that gets 0 waits for you.

Checking

A food layer checks every pick.

Before a link counts, plain rules look at it. A line sold in litres cannot be a solid like cheese or chocolate, and the pack size has to make sense for the food. Bags and detergent are set aside as non-food.

A Swiss glossary adds words like Rüebli, Nüsslisalat and Peperoni, plus common French and Italian food words, to the search and to the pick prompt. Quantities come from the line itself, converted by code.

Privacy

Air-gapped by default.

A year of shopping says a lot about health, religion and money. Under the revised Swiss data protection act that is sensitive personal data, so it stays on your machine.

make airgap puts the app and a llama.cpp model server on a Docker network marked internal, with no route out. Before it starts, the app tries four outside hosts and refuses to run if any of them answers. There is no account and no telemetry. If you would rather use a Swiss cloud, point LLM_BASE_URL at it.

Around the model

The parts that make it usable.

Migros CSV and Coop PDF

Import the Cumulus CSV export from Migros, or the text of a Coop PDF receipt.

Pasted text

Paste lines from any Swiss receipt and they go through the same reader.

Four languages

Receipts in German, French and Italian. The weekly suggestion in those three and English.

A Swiss food glossary

Rüebli, Nüsslisalat, Peperoni and common French and Italian food words, added to search and to the pick prompt.

The weekly pyramid

Shares of the Swiss food pyramid per adult-equivalent, with sugar, salt, saturated fat and fruit and vegetable portions.

One suggestion a week

Apertus writes the sentence and code fills in the numbers through placeholders. In German, code also turns ß into ss, the Swiss spelling.

An air gap you can test

make airgap refuses to start if any of four outside hosts answers.

A linker you can reuse

The linker is one small Python module, src/bonappetit/linker.py, usable on its own.

Fixes for open data

An Atwater check flags 3,035 of 86,517 Swiss products on Open Food Facts (3.5%) whose printed calories contradict their macronutrients; 433 look like kJ typed as kcal. The list is in eval/off_energy_flags.csv.

Baselines.

Fuzzy string matching, the way a 2018 hackathon app did it. Embeddings alone. Apertus alone, asked to guess the nutrients with no database. Then the full hybrid.

The oracle row is the best score any FSVO match could reach. FSVO holds generic foods and the packs are specific brands, so even a perfect link has some error.

Limits.

Purchases are not intake. Food gets shared, thrown away or eaten outside the house, and a receipt shows none of that.

The rules that turn product names into receipt-style truncations are synthetic, and the report documents them. FSVO values are generic, so a brand's recipe can differ.

Prior work.

TooSweet, from the Open Food Hackdays in 2018, ranked Cumulus purchases by sugar. ETH Zurich's FoodCoach and Receipt2Nutrition research studied nutrition from shopping data before us.

Bon·Appétit adds a local model that can abstain, and a check layer behind it.

The list, for Open Food Facts volunteers

What Apertus taught us.

  1. Swiss German needs help.

    The 8B model read RUEBLI, Swiss German for carrot, as kale and once as flatbread. A short glossary of Swiss food words now goes into the search and the prompt.

  2. Spelling instructions slip.

    Asked for Swiss spelling, it still wrote ß in German suggestions. Code converts it to ss.

  3. A strict grammar made it worse.

    A full JSON schema grammar made each call about four times slower and made the model answer "no match" for almost every line. Plain JSON mode, with the schema checked in code, fixed both.

  4. It rarely says it is sure.

    So the app does not ask it for a confidence score. It chooses a list number, or 0.

Who it is for

Households first, then the people who help them.

Households

See what a week of shopping adds up to, privately, with swaps you can make in the same shop.

Dietitians and home care

Start from what a client actually bought, with their consent, instead of a food diary written from memory.

Insurer prevention programmes

Offer members a weekly view that stays on their own computer, so the receipts never reach the insurer.

Retailers' loyalty apps

Run the linker next to the purchase data a loyalty programme already holds, with the Swiss pyramid as the reference.

Cantonal health promotion

Use the open code in nutrition programmes in German, French or Italian speaking cantons.

Researchers

Reuse the linker module and the label-based evaluation to study shopping data and diet.

Run it

Three commands on your own computer.

You need git and Docker. Everything runs in a container on your machine.

Terminal
$ git clone https://github.com/kinnuworks/bon-appetit
$ cd bon-appetit
$ make run

make airgap runs the app with no route out. make eval re-scores the stored predictions and checks their SHA-256.

Swiss cloud instead
$ export LLM_NAME=swiss-ai/Apertus-v1.5-8B
$ export LLM_BASE_URL=https://your-swiss-endpoint/v1
$ export LLM_API_KEY=your-key
$ make run

With these set, receipt lines go to that endpoint instead of the local model. Pick a provider that runs in Switzerland under Swiss law.

Questions

Honest answers.

Is this medical advice?

No. It describes what a household bought and compares that with the Swiss dietary recommendations. For a diagnosis or a diet plan, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian.

Does anything leave my computer?

Not with make airgap. The app and the model server sit on a Docker network marked internal, with no route out, and the app refuses to start if any of four outside hosts answers. There is no account and no telemetry. If you set LLM_BASE_URL, the receipt lines go to that endpoint, so choose one you trust.

Why not just use barcodes?

The Migros Cumulus export has no barcodes, and a paper receipt does not print them. The shortened product name is all there is to work with.

What about Coop?

Coop PDF receipts are read as text. Lines with Coop house brands such as Naturaplan, Qualité & Prix and Prix Garantie go through the same reader as Migros lines.

What if it gets an item wrong?

Every line shows the FSVO food it was linked to, so a wrong link is easy to spot. The demo on this page keeps the real mistakes, such as iced tea linked to lemon juice. When no candidate fits, Apertus answers 0 and the line waits for you, and the food layer rejects links whose unit or pack size makes no sense.

Which model runs it?

Apertus v1.5 8B, running locally; the air-gapped setup serves it with llama.cpp. It is called twice per line, once to read the line and once to pick a candidate by its number. It never produces a nutrient value: code computes every quantity from the FSVO database.

Can I use it in French or Italian?

Yes. It reads receipts in German, French and Italian, and writes the weekly suggestion in German, French, Italian or English.

How accurate is it?

See the evidence section. Estimates are measured against the nutrition labels printed on Swiss products on Open Food Facts, next to simpler baselines and an oracle that shows the best any FSVO match could do. What a household buys is still not exactly what it eats.
